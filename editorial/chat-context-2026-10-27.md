## What counts as repetition?

**Potentially productive callback:** A return to Adam, the fall, `shamar`, the garden, the heart, the father, the names, Odysseus or the castle that deepens, complicates or fulfils earlier material.

**Potentially unproductive repetition:** A passage that makes a later chapter start again at Genesis by explaining the already-established premise with no significant new dimension.

For every suspected repeat, record:

- The **exact paragraph or short identifying quotation** in the source chapter.
- Earlier and later passages covering related ground.
- The **new work**, if any, that this occurrence performs.
- What the argument would lose if removed or moved.
- Whether a callback can be retained with less exposition.
- Your proposed verdict: **KEEP / CONDENSE / MOVE / EXPAND / REMOVE**.
- Your confidence and any theological, literary, structural or reader-experience risks.

**Do not equate repeated vocabulary or themes with repeated meaning.**

## Chapter 6: significant authorial intentions

The **Odysseus suitors** section depicts an estate still functioning and producing while intruders consume its fruit. The image matters because a man's home and life can seem outwardly intact while governed and consumed under the wrong authority. This connects to the inner castle and the eventual restoration of the household.

The **war for attention** is specifically about **divide and conquer**. Its central concern is a man's distracted attention allowing the ground he was entrusted to guard to go untended, even while he is physically present at home.

The **Juvenal scene** is purposefully immersive: a reader imagines himself among a Roman crowd attending the arena. It is *deliberate literary staging*, not a claim that the author has documentary evidence Juvenal sat in a particular seat. Do not interrupt the author's prose to make an irrelevant point about historical dramatization.

The author revised the closing words of the Juvenal passage to:

> Nothing has really changed. We recognise the enemy on distant battlefields and in other people's lives, while he takes ground inside our own homes. And like the crowds in Rome, we sit comfortably entertained and numbed, scarcely noticing what we were meant to *shamar* slipping from our hands.

The one agreed grammatical correction was to add **"in"** before **"other people's lives"**.

The reference to distant battlefields consciously recalls earlier text *in Chapter 6* about imagining evil as remote, e.g. foreign wars, while failing to perceive the same enemy operating close to home. **Do not spell out the callback** with artificial narration like "Earlier we spoke about ..." The author explicitly rejected that approach.

In an earlier edit the assistant overwrote a user-written Roman arena paragraph despite a request to change only the one flagged phrase. The author repeatedly corrected this. **Never silently replace user prose with an assistant's previous rewrite.** Exact wording matters.
